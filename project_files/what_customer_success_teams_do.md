## PART 1 — Customer Success in General

### 1.1 What it fundamentally is

Customer Success (CS) owns the customer relationship **after the sale**. Its single purpose: make sure customers get real value from what they bought, so they **stay, renew, and buy more**.

The discipline grew out of the SaaS / subscription world, where the company only makes money if customers keep paying month after month. That changed the economics of customer relationships: keeping an existing customer successful became directly tied to revenue, not just goodwill. Acquiring a customer is expensive and happens once; retaining and expanding them happens continuously and is where the profit compounds.

### 1.2 The one distinction that defines CS: proactive vs. reactive

This is the heart of it, and the line most worth being able to say cleanly:

- **Customer Support is reactive.** It waits for a ticket, then fixes the problem. Success = problem resolved.
- **Customer Success is proactive.** It reaches out *before* there's a problem — spots the account going quiet, the usage dropping, the renewal approaching — and intervenes early. Success = the customer thrives and never had to complain.

A CS team that's only firefighting tickets isn't really doing CS; it's doing support with a nicer title. Real CS is anticipatory.

### 1.3 The core responsibilities

**Onboarding.**
Getting a customer from "just signed" to "up, running, and getting value." Setup, configuration, training, and driving to *first value* as fast as possible. This phase matters disproportionately: most churn risk is seeded in the first few weeks, when a customer either forms the habit or quietly stalls.

**Adoption & engagement.**
Making sure the customer actually uses the product — and uses the parts that create value. A customer who pays but doesn't use is a customer about to leave. CS monitors usage, runs enablement, and nudges users toward the features that make them stick.

**Retention & renewals.**
The headline job. When the contract comes up, does the customer stay? CS is measured on **churn** (customers/revenue lost) and **retention**. Renewals are often where CS formally "owns the number."

**Expansion / upsell / cross-sell.**
Growing existing accounts — more seats, more sites, upgraded tiers, additional products. This is usually the cheapest revenue a company has, because the customer already trusts you. In mature orgs, expansion revenue from the existing base can exceed new-logo revenue.

**Proactive account management.**
Regular check-ins, and the classic **QBR (Quarterly Business Review)** — the CSM sits with the client, demonstrates the value delivered ("here's what you got from us this quarter"), surfaces risks, and plans ahead. This is where CS proves ROI and deepens the relationship.

**Voice of the customer.**
CS sits closest to the customer's real experience, so it feeds insight back to **Product** (what to build), **Sales** (what's landing, what's not), and **Marketing** (what stories are true). A good CS team is the company's early-warning system.

**Advocacy & references.**
Turning happy customers into references, case studies, and referrals — which lowers acquisition cost for the whole company.

### 1.4 How CS measures itself

- **Churn rate** — % of customers or revenue lost in a period. The number CS most fears.
- **Retention rate** — the inverse; % kept.
- **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)** — revenue from existing customers over time *including* expansion and minus churn. NRR above 100% means the existing base grows even with zero new customers — the gold-standard CS metric.
- **Health score** — a composite, at-a-glance signal of how at-risk or healthy an account is. Built from inputs like product usage/engagement, support-ticket history, sentiment, contract value, and time-to-renewal. Health scores are how CS teams *triage* — deciding which accounts need attention today.
- **NPS (Net Promoter Score)** — would the customer recommend you? A sentiment proxy.
- **Time-to-value / time-to-first-value** — how fast a new customer reaches their first real win. Shorter is better; long TTV predicts churn.
- **Adoption / activation rate** — how deeply the product is actually used.

### 1.5 The tools CS typically runs on

CS work is notoriously spread across tools — which is exactly the pain Aquablu's brief describes. A typical stack:

- **CRM** (e.g., HubSpot, Salesforce) — accounts, contacts, deals, renewal dates, account value.
- **A dedicated CS platform** (e.g., Gainsight, ChurnZero, Vitally, Planhat) — health scores, playbooks, renewal tracking — *if* the company has matured into one. Many scale-ups haven't, and fake it with the CRM plus spreadsheets.
- **Support / ticketing** (e.g., Zendesk, Intercom) — reactive issues.
- **Product-usage / analytics data** — often in a separate BI tool or the product itself.
- **Email, Slack, and spreadsheets** — the universal glue, where context actually lives.

The structural problem: **the account context is in the CRM, the usage truth is in the product/analytics, the issues are in the ticketing tool, and the plan is in a spreadsheet** — so a CSM assessing one account has to mentally join four sources. That fragmentation is the classic CS pain, and it's precisely what the Aquablu case is about.

### 1.6 Common CS roles

- **CSM (Customer Success Manager)** — owns a book of accounts; the relationship, retention, and expansion for those accounts.
- **Onboarding / Implementation Specialist** — owns the first phase, getting customers live.
- **CS Operations** — builds the systems, health scores, and reporting the team runs on.
- **Team Lead / Head of CS** — strategy, targets, and team management.
- **(Adjacent) Support / Technical Support** — reactive issue resolution, often confused with or blended into CS.

## PART 2 — Customer Success at Aquablu Specifically

> Aquablu is **not** pure SaaS. This is the single most important thing to internalize, because it means the Part 1 textbook applies only partially, and the *differences* are where your business case gets interesting.

### 2.1 What makes Aquablu's CS unusual

Aquablu sells **hardware (dispensers) + consumables (flavors, CO₂, filters) + a platform (AURA)** on a **subscription / pay-per-drink** model. That physical dimension adds responsibilities a software CS team never has:

- **Onboarding is a physical install**, not a login. A new customer means a certified technician connecting and commissioning a dispenser on-site (Aquablu says this usually takes about an hour), with prior advice on placement (standalone vs. built-in) and the mains water connection.
- **Consumable & service logistics** — flavors, CO₂, filters, scheduled maintenance, and dispatching field **Service Engineers**.
- **Adoption is literal** — the pay-per-drink subscription model means revenue depends on the number of people using the machine.

## 2. What CS Almost Certainly Does at Aquablu (hypotheses to validate)

Aquablu is not classic SaaS — it's **hardware + consumables + a platform, on a subscription/pay-per-drink model.** That shape means their CS work is a hybrid of software-CS and physical-product account management. Grounded in Aquablu's own published material, here's the likely lifecycle CS touches:

**a) Onboarding a new client site.** Aquablu's Sales Executive posting says sales works "with marketing, customer success and Ops to ensure onboarding goes well." Onboarding a client isn't a software login — it's coordinating a physical install: *"Our certified technicians handle the complete connection and commissioning, usually within one hour,"* with pre-advice on placement (standalone vs. built-in), furniture options, and the mains connection. So CS likely orchestrates: contract handoff from sales → site survey/placement advice → install scheduling with Service Engineers → flavor selection → go-live → first-weeks adoption check.

**b) Driving adoption & engagement.** The whole business model depends on people actually *using* the machine (pay-per-drink revenue + retention). CS likely runs launch activations, monitors early usage via AURA, and intervenes when a site's dispense volume is low (a leading indicator of churn). Aquablu literally sells "engagement" as a use case.

**c) Account health, renewals & upsell.** Subscription model = renewals and expansion (more units, more sites, new products like BOLD). CS owns retention. Aquablu boasts customers "keep coming back and recommend Aquablu without being asked" — that loyalty is CS's KPI.

**d) Reactive support & issue resolution.** Handling client questions, flavor/CO₂ reorder requests, complaints, and triaging technical issues to Service Engineers. Aquablu's own operational stats reveal the support reality: *"Over 99% of anomalies auto-resolve before a service ticket fires; 80% of errors clear within 24 hours, 92% within a week; two scheduled maintenance visits per year."* So AURA already prevents most tickets — meaning human CS time goes to the *exceptions* and the *relationships*, not routine breakage.

**e) Sustainability/ESG reporting to clients.** CS likely packages AURA's bottle-saved / CO₂ numbers into reviews clients show their leadership (the Adyen "1.2M bottles by 2030" type figure). Quarterly business reviews built on AURA data are a plausible CS ritual.

**f) Being the voice of the customer internally.** Feeding client feedback to product/marketing.
